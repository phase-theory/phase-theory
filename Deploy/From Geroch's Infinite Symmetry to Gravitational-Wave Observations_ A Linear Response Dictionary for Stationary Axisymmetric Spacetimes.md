# From Geroch's Infinite Symmetry to Gravitational-Wave Observations: A Linear Response Dictionary for Stationary Axisymmetric Spacetimes

## Mathematical Foundations of the Geroch Group

The study of exact solutions to the Einstein field equations has long been a cornerstone of theoretical physics, providing invaluable insights into the nature of gravity beyond perturbative regimes. Among the most powerful techniques for generating such solutions are solution-generating methods, which leverage hidden symmetries of the equations to construct new spacetimes from known ones [[18](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/A-Method-for-Generating-New-Solutions-of-Einstein%27s-Geroch/740d10b1771c684025fb504c1143f79ca3b02f8e)]. The focus of this report is the Geroch group, an infinite-dimensional symmetry group that acts on the space of stationary, axisymmetric vacuum spacetimes [[17](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/JHEP06(2025)064), [76](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/261136610_The_Geroch_group_in_Einstein_spaces)]. This section provides a comprehensive exposition of the mathematical structure underpinning this group, tracing its origin from the reduction of the four-dimensional vacuum Einstein equations to a two-dimensional integrable system. Our goal is to build a self-contained foundation, balancing rigor with accessibility for a multidisciplinary audience, by first establishing the reduced field equations and then systematically constructing the associated algebraic and geometric machinery.

The starting point for this construction is the class of stationary, axisymmetric spacetimes. These geometries possess two commuting Killing vector fields, one timelike ($\xi^\mu$) and one spacelike and hypersurface-orthogonal ($\eta^\mu$), corresponding to time translation and axial rotation symmetries, respectively. In the canonical Lewis-Papapetrou form, the line element can be written as $ds^2 = f^{-1} e^{2\nu} (dt - \omega d\phi)^2 - f (d\rho^2 + dz^2) - f^{-1} \rho^2 d\phi^2$, where $f, \nu, \omega$ are functions only of the Weyl coordinates $(\rho, z)$ [[20](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.03675)]. The vacuum Einstein equations, $R_{\mu\nu} = 0$, reduce to a set of coupled nonlinear partial differential equations for these three potentials. This system can be elegantly reformulated as a two-dimensional non-linear sigma model, where the metric coefficients map the flat $(\rho, z)$ plane into a three-dimensional target space with a specific geometry [[17](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/JHEP06(2025)064)].

A more profound simplification arises through the introduction of the Ernst potential, $\mathcal{E} = \Phi + \frac{1}{2} \epsilon_{\kappa\lambda\mu\nu} \xi^\kappa \eta^\lambda \nabla^\mu \xi^\nu$. Here, $\Phi$ is the twist potential defined via $d\Phi = \star_3 (d\xi \wedge d\eta)$, where $\star_3$ is the Hodge dual on the three-dimensional quotient space [[17](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/JHEP06(2025)064)]. The complex Ernst potential, $\mathcal{E}$, encapsulates the gravitational degrees of freedom and the reduction of the Einstein equations becomes the single Ernst equation . For a real parameter $\alpha$, the Ehlers transformation is given by $\mathcal{E}' = \frac{\mathcal{E} - i\alpha}{\mathcal{E} + i\alpha}$. This action corresponds to an $SL(2,\mathbb{R})$ symmetry of the field equations. While powerful, the generic application of this transformation introduces a NUT (Newman–Unti–Tamburino) charge and can spoil the condition of asymptotic flatness, which is a crucial requirement for describing astrophysical black holes [[17](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/JHEP06(2025)064)]. A second, distinct $SL(2,\mathbb{R})$ symmetry was later identified by Matzner and Misner [[17](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/JHEP06(2025)064)]. Their transformation acts not on the Ernst potential directly, but on the metric components in a different formulation, specifically the Kaluza-Klein reduction along the axial Killing direction. In this picture, one constructs a symmetric matrix $\mathcal{M}$ from the metric potentials, which transforms under the adjoint action of another $SL(2,\mathbb{R})$ group [[17](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/JHEP06(2025)064)]. This Matzner-Misner transformation mixes the conformal factor with the gravitational potentials in a manner distinct from the Ehlers action, offering a complementary way to deform a seed solution.

Geroch's seminal 1972 paper demonstrated that these two $SL(2,\mathbb{R})$ transformations do not generate a finite-dimensional group; rather, their repeated alternation generates an infinite-dimensional Lie group, now known as the Geroch group [[18](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/A-Method-for-Generating-New-Solutions-of-Einstein%27s-Geroch/740d10b1771c684025fb504c1143f79ca3b02f8e)]. The structure of this group was subsequently clarified by Breitenlohner and Maison, who showed that it is an affine Kac-Moody extension of the loop algebra of $SL(2,\mathbb{R})$ [[17](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/JHEP06(2025)064)]. To understand this, one must introduce a complex spectral parameter, $\lambda \in \mathbb{C}$, which satisfies a reality condition related to the signature of the spacetime. The key insight is that the Ernst equation itself can be recast as the compatibility condition for a linear system of first-order differential equations, often called the zero-curvature or Lax pair representation. This linear system takes the form $\partial_\nu A_\mu - \partial_\mu A_\nu + [A_\mu, A_\nu] = 0$, where the connection matrices $A_\mu$ take values in the Lie algebra of $SL(2,\mathbb{R})$ and depend rationally on the spectral parameter $\lambda$ [[17](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/JHEP06(2025)064)]. The existence of such a linear system is the hallmark of a completely integrable system, a concept central to the field of integrable Hamiltonian systems [[50](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/barbaresco_symplectic-geometry-of-integrable-hamiltonian-activity-7398309369622245376-4CiD), [51](https://zuchongzhi.dukekunshan.edu.cn/research-areas/)].

The affine Kac-Moody structure emerges naturally from this linear system. The group elements of the Geroch group can be represented as loops, i.e., maps from the circle (parameterized by $\lambda$ on the unit circle $|\lambda|=1$) into the group $SL(2,\mathbb{R})$. The infinitesimal generators of the group are therefore Laurent series in $\lambda$, $g(\lambda) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} g_n \lambda^n$, where each coefficient $g_n$ is an element of the $sl(2,\mathbb{R})$ Lie algebra. The commutation relations of these generators, $[g_m, h_n] = [g_0, h_0]\delta_{m+n,0} + m k \delta_{m+n,0} \text{Tr}(g_0 h_0)$, where $k$ is the level of the representation, define the affine Kac-Moody algebra. The appearance of the central term, proportional to the trace of the generators, is a characteristic feature of this algebraic structure and is essential for its consistency. This infinite-dimensional algebra contains the original two $SL(2,\mathbb{R})$ factors as subalgebras corresponding to the constant and simple pole terms in the Laurent expansion. The existence of an infinite number of conserved charges associated with this algebra, whose Poisson brackets generate a Yangian symmetry, further underscores the profound integrability of the system [[31](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.07662), [38](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.07527), [39](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/330552979_Integrability_of_anti-self-dual_vacuum_Einstein_equations_with_nonzero_cosmological_constant_an_infinite_hierarchy_of_nonlocal_conservation_laws)].

The practical utility of this formalism for generating new solutions lies in the Riemann-Hilbert problem. Given a "seed" solution (e.g., the Kerr solution), a new solution can be constructed by "dressing" the seed's linear system with a group element, represented by a loop $v(\lambda)$. This process involves solving a factorization problem on a contour in the complex $\lambda$-plane, typically the unit circle. One seeks to decompose a given jump matrix, $J(\lambda)$, which encodes the parameters of the desired deformation, into a product of two matrices, $v_+(\lambda)$ and $v_-(\lambda)$, analytic inside and outside the unit circle, respectively: $J(\lambda) = v_+(\lambda) v_-(\lambda)^{-1}$ for $\lambda$ on the unit circle. The new connection matrices are then constructed from the analytic pieces, $A'_\mu = v_+ A_\mu v_+^{-1} - (\partial_\mu v_+) v_+^{-1}$. Integrating these new connections reconstructs the dressed Ernst potential, thereby yielding a new exact solution to the vacuum Einstein equations [[17](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/JHEP06(2025)064)]. This Riemann-Hilbert factorization provides a systematic, albeit computationally intensive, algorithm for exploring the vast landscape of stationary, axisymmetric spacetimes generated by the Geroch group. The entire mathematical edifice, from the reduced sigma model to the affine Kac-Moody algebra, provides the rigorous framework upon which the mapping to physical observables will be built in subsequent sections.

## Physical Subgroups and the Riemann-Hilbert Factorization Pipeline

While the abstract Geroch group offers a vast algebraic playground for generating new solutions, not all of its elements correspond to physically relevant spacetimes. To construct a theory applicable to astrophysical black holes, it is imperative to identify the subgroup of the Geroch group that preserves the fundamental physical properties of the seed solution, namely asymptotic flatness and the regularity of the axis of symmetry. The initial manuscript draft touches upon this by noting that generic Ehlers transformations inject NUT charge and spoil asymptotic flatness [[17](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/JHEP06(2025)064)]. This section elaborates on these constraints, defines the physical subgroup precisely, and outlines the numerical pipeline for implementing the Riemann-Hilbert factorization to generate explicit, physically admissible deformed spacetimes.

The primary physical requirements for a stationary, axisymmetric black hole spacetime are twofold. First, the spacetime must be asymptotically flat, meaning that at large distances, the metric approaches the Minkowski metric. This imposes strong constraints on the behavior of the metric potentials $f, \nu, \omega$ as $\rho^2+z^2 \to \infty$. Specifically, they must fall off sufficiently fast, typically requiring $f \to 1$, $\nu \to 0$, and $\omega \to 0$. Second, the spacetime must be free of a NUT charge, which is characterized by a specific logarithmic divergence in the time-component of the metric at infinity. A non-zero NUT charge also violates the standard notion of energy at infinity and introduces pathological global structures, such as closed timelike curves near the axis. Third, the axis of symmetry ($\rho=0$) must be "elementarily flat," meaning that the geometry there resembles that of a smooth axis in flat space, which constrains the behavior of the potentials as $\rho \to 0$.

These physical requirements translate directly into constraints on the allowed group elements within the Geroch group. The NUT-free, asymptotically flat subgroup, denoted $\mathcal{G}_{phys}$, consists of those loop group elements $v(\lambda)$ that satisfy several conditions [[17](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/JHEP06(2025)064)]. These include reality conditions ensuring the final metric is real, a normalization condition like $\det(v(\lambda))=1$, and crucially, a condition related to the moments of the spectral parameter. Specifically, to preserve asymptotic flatness and eliminate NUT charge, the group element must be chosen such that its odd imaginary moments vanish [[17](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/JHEP06(2025)064)]. This constraint effectively restricts the Laurent series expansion of the group element. For instance, a physically admissible dressing transformation might be restricted to loops that are holomorphic (have no negative powers of $\lambda$) everywhere except possibly at infinity. This restriction ensures that the resulting solution does not develop the pathologies associated with the full, unconstrained group. Within this physical subgroup, a near-identity flow is parameterized by a two-parameter family, allowing for controlled deformations away from the seed Kerr solution [[17](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/JHEP06(2025)064)]. This physically motivated truncation of the infinite-dimensional group is the key step that connects the abstract mathematics to a concrete, testable physical model.

With the physical subgroup defined, the next step is to implement the generation of solutions numerically. The Riemann-Hilbert factorization problem, while conceptually clear, presents significant computational challenges. The goal is to find the analytic pieces $v_+(\lambda)$ and $v_-(\lambda)$ from their boundary values related by the jump matrix $J(\lambda)$. This is typically formulated as a nonlinear singular integral equation on the unit circle. A robust numerical pipeline for solving this problem, as outlined in the preliminary analysis and mentioned in Section 23 of the original manuscript, proceeds as follows:

1.  **Construct the Seed Data:** Begin with the known Ernst potential and metric components of the seed solution, such as the Kerr spacetime. This provides the unperturbed background against which the deformation will be computed [[17](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/JHEP06(2025)064)].
2.  **Specify the Deformation Parameters:** Choose the parameters of the Geroch flow, defining the jump matrix $J(\lambda)$. For a near-identity flow, this matrix is a small perturbation of the identity matrix, parameterized by the Ehlers and Matzner-Misner parameters [[17](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/JHEP06(2025)064)]. The choice of parameters determines the nature of the resulting deformation.
3.  **Discretize the Problem:** The integral equation on the unit circle is discretized using a spectral method for high accuracy. A common and efficient approach is to place grid points on the unit circle using a Chebyshev grid. This leverages the smoothness of the solution to achieve exponential convergence of the numerical result [[17](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/JHEP06(2025)064)].
4.  **Solve the Nonlinear System:** The discretized integral equation is a system of nonlinear algebraic equations for the values of the analytic matrices at the grid points. This system is typically solved using an iterative method, such as Newton's method, starting from an initial guess (e.g., the trivial solution corresponding to the seed metric). Each iteration involves matrix multiplications and inversions.
5.  **Reconstruct the Dressed Potential:** Once the analytic pieces $v_+(\lambda)$ and $v_-(\lambda)$ are known numerically, the new connection matrices $A'_\mu$ can be constructed. By integrating these connections along a path from a reference point (e.g., spatial infinity) to any point $(\rho, z)$ in the Weyl cylinder, the dressed Ernst potential $\mathcal{E}'(\rho, z)$ can be reconstructed. This integration step may require another numerical procedure, such as a line integral solver.
6.  **Compute Metric Components:** Finally, from the dressed Ernst potential, the other metric potentials ($f, \nu, \omega$) can be derived. This completes the generation of the fully deformed, physically admissible spacetime metric. This numerical pipeline forms the backbone of the framework, transforming the abstract Riemann-Hilbert problem into a concrete, calculable tool for exploring the consequences of Geroch symmetry.

This pipeline allows for the systematic exploration of the Geroch group's physical subgroup. For example, applying a small Ehlers flow to the Kerr seed generates a new solution with a modified mass aspect and a non-zero quadrupole moment, while preserving the mass and angular momentum to first order after a compensating coordinate rescaling [[17](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/JHEP06(2025)064)]. Similarly, a Matzner-Misner flow modifies the mass and quadrupole moments in a different, asymmetric way [[17](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/JHEP06(2025)064)]. The numerical implementation enables the precise computation of these changes, paving the way for the calculation of observable effects in the next section.

## The Observable Dictionary: Geodesic and Curvature-Based Quantities

The ultimate purpose of generating new spacetimes is to predict their physical consequences and compare them with observations. This section bridges the gap between the abstract, mathematically generated Geroch-deformed spacetimes and the concrete quantities measured by distant observers and gravitational-wave detectors. We construct an "observable dictionary," a finite vector of measurable quantities, and detail how to compute their first-order shifts under an infinitesimal Geroch flow. This process defines the linear response kernels, which are the central objects mapping group parameters to physical predictions. We focus here on observables derived from geodesics and spacetime curvature.

The proposed observable vector $\mathcal{O}$ is composed of several key channels of information [[17](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/JHEP06(2025)064)]:
$$
\mathcal{O} = \left( z_{ZAMO}, I_2, I_3, \Omega_{ISCO}, b_{LR}, \omega_{QNM}, T_H, \kappa_H, A_H \right)
$$
where each component represents a physically measurable quantity. We begin with the observer channel, which probes the local gravitational environment.

The first observable is the redshift measured by a Zero-Angular-Momentum Observer (ZAMO). A ZAMO is a fiducial observer hovering at fixed spatial coordinates $(\rho, z)$, with a four-velocity proportional to the timelike Killing vector, $u^\mu = (-g_{tt})^{-1/2} \delta^\mu_t$. The redshift $z_{ZAMO}$ of a photon received from infinity is given by $1+z_{ZAMO} = (-(g_{tt}))^{-1/2}$ [[11](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35173346/), [12](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/368716354_A_unified_treatment_of_the_redshift_the_Doppler_effect_and_the_time_dilation_in_general_relativity_and_its_applications)]. Under a small perturbation of the metric, $\delta g_{\mu\nu}$, induced by a Geroch flow, the first-order shift in the redshift is simply:
$$
\delta z_{ZAMO} = \frac{1}{2} z_{ZAMO} (1+z_{ZAMO})^2 \delta g_{tt}
$$
This establishes the response kernel for the redshift as being proportional to the time-time component of the metric perturbation. This perturbation, $\delta g_{\mu\nu}$, is computed by applying the numerical Riemann-Hilbert pipeline to the seed Kerr metric with specified Ehlers or Matzner-Misner parameters [[17](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/JHEP06(2025)064)].

The next two observables, $I_2$ and $I_3$, are quadratic and cubic invariants of the Weyl curvature tensor, respectively. They serve as diagnostics for the departure of the spacetime from Kerr, which is characterized by a specific pattern of curvature. The quadratic invariant is $I_2 = C_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta} C^{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}$, and the cubic invariant is $I_3 = \epsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta} C^{\alpha\beta\rho\sigma} C_{\rho\sigma\mu\nu} C^{\mu\nu\lambda\tau} \epsilon_{\lambda\tau}^{\ \ \alpha\beta} / \sqrt{-g}$ [[24](https://jwc.fudan.edu.cn/_upload/article/files/1d/3e/5da3a3c14c8a9b5722e0f99fcda2/42331381-f715-45f2-9cb7-7d2a3ec71857.pdf)]. Both invariants are constructed from the Weyl tensor, which depends on the metric and its first two derivatives. Consequently, their response kernels are complex functionals involving the first and second derivatives of the metric perturbation, $\delta g_{\mu\nu}$. Computing these shifts requires evaluating the Riemann tensor for the dressed metric and then contracting it to form the invariants, a process that can be automated once the deformed metric is known.

Moving to the detector channel, we consider observables derived from circular equatorial geodesics. The innermost stable circular orbit (ISCO) is a critical radius in the accretion disk around a black hole, marking the transition from inspiral to plunge [[3](https://cosmo.nyu.edu/yacine/teaching/GR_2018/lectures/lecture22.pdf)]. Its location and orbital frequency are key parameters in modeling the late-inspiral phase of a binary coalescence. The ISCO is found by solving two conditions on the effective potential for radial motion, $V_{eff}(r)$: $dV_{eff}/dr = 0$ (a circular orbit) and $d^2V_{eff}/dr^2 = 0$ (an orbit at the threshold of stability) [[2](https://academic.oup.com/mnras/article-pdf/422/3/2581/18460016/mnras0422-2581.pdf), [57](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.02638v1)]. For a particle of mass $m$ and angular momentum $L$, the effective potential in the equatorial plane is $V_{eff}(r) = -\frac{1}{2} m^2 (g_{tt} + \frac{L^2}{r^2} g_{\phi\phi})$ [[57](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.02638v1)]. Varying these conditions to first order yields a linear equation for the shift in the ISCO radius, $\delta r_{ISCO}$, in terms of the metric perturbation $\delta g_{\mu\nu}$. The ISCO frequency, $\Omega_{ISCO} = d\phi/dt = \sqrt{-g_{tt}/g_{\phi\phi}} \cdot \sqrt{M/r_{ISCO}^3}$, then acquires a shift $\delta\Omega_{ISCO}$ that is a functional of $\delta r_{ISCO}$ and the metric perturbation evaluated at $r_{ISCO}$ [[1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1605.04189)]. This defines the response kernel for the ISCO frequency.

Similarly, the prograde light ring—the radius of the unstable null circular orbit—is another crucial observable, strongly imprinted in the ringdown phase of a gravitational waveform [[84](https://arxiv.org/abs/1609.00083)]. It is found by setting $dV_{eff}/dr = 0$ for null geodesics, where the effective potential simplifies to $V_{eff}(r) = \frac{1}{2} L^2/r^2 g_{\phi\phi}$ [[57](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.02638v1)]. The corresponding impact parameter, $b = L/E$, gives the apparent size of the black hole's shadow. As with the ISCO, the position of the light ring, $r_{LR}$, and its associated impact parameter, $b_{LR}$, shift under a Geroch deformation. The first-order response kernels for these quantities are derived by linearizing the equation for the light-ring position. A key finding is that for a near-identity Ehlers flow, the ISCO frequency and light-ring radius respond degenerately to certain rescalings, but this degeneracy is broken when compared to the response of these same observables to a Matzner-Misner flow, which produces relative shifts of opposite sign [[17](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/JHEP06(2025)064)]. This property is crucial for breaking parameter degeneracies in data analysis.

## The Observable Dictionary: Quasinormal Modes and Horizon Thermodynamics

Beyond observables derived from geodesics, the late-stage evolution of a black hole merger—known as the ringdown phase—is dominated by its characteristic quasinormal modes (QNMs). These are complex frequencies that encode the properties of the final object's spacetime geometry. Additionally, the event horizon itself possesses thermodynamic properties, such as area and surface gravity, which are linked by the laws of black hole mechanics. This section details the response kernels for these horizon-centric observables, completing the dictionary and enabling a full characterization of the Geroch-deformed spacetimes.

Quasinormal modes are the characteristic vibrational frequencies of a black hole, analogous to the decayed chimes of a bell. After a disturbance, such as the merger of two black holes, the final remnant settles into a stationary state by emitting gravitational waves at these discrete, complex frequencies $\omega = \omega_R + i\omega_I$. The real part, $\omega_R$, is the oscillation frequency, while the inverse of the imaginary part, $-\omega_I$, sets the damping timescale. For a Kerr black hole, these modes are solutions to the Teukolsky equation, a master equation governing the perturbations of various fields [[4](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/1908655_Method_to_estimate_ISCO_and_ring-down_frequencies_in_binary_systems_and_consequences_for_gravitational_wave_data_analysis)]. The QNMs are uniquely determined by the black hole's mass and spin, making them a direct probe of its multipolar structure. The response kernel for the QNM frequency, $K_{QNM}$, maps the metric deformation $\delta g_{\mu\nu}$ generated by a Geroch flow to the first-order shift in the complex frequency, $\delta\omega$.

The calculation of this kernel is intricate. The Geroch deformation alters the background Kerr metric, which in turn modifies the effective potential in the Teukolsky equation. The first-order shift in this potential, $\delta V(r)$, is a functional of the metric perturbation $\delta g_{\mu\nu}$ and its derivatives. The shift in the QNM frequency, $\delta\omega$, is then obtained by applying first-order perturbation theory to the eigenvalue problem defined by the Teukolsky equation. A powerful method for computing QNMs is Leaver's continued fraction approach, which reformulates the problem into a recurrence relation for the coefficients of a series solution [[4](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/1908655_Method_to_estimate_ISCO_and_ring-down_frequencies_in_binary_systems_and_consequences_for_gravitational_wave_data_analysis)]. To first order, this method can be adapted to solve for $\delta\omega$ given the perturbed potential. The resulting expression for $\delta\omega$ constitutes the desired response kernel. This connects the abstract algebraic parameter of the Geroch group directly to the dominant frequency and damping time observed in the ringdown portion of a gravitational-wave signal [[74](https://arxiv.org/abs/gr-qc/0605118)]. The ability to compute this shift analytically, even if implicitly, is a significant achievement of the framework.

The second set of horizon-based observables relates to black hole thermodynamics. According to the laws of black hole mechanics, the horizon area $A$ is proportional to the entropy $S$, and the surface gravity $\kappa$ is proportional to the temperature $T_H$ [[7](https://www.mdpi.com/1099-4300/22/11/1246), [55](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/403511515_Dynamical_Black_Hole_Thermodynamics_in_Modified_Gravity)]. These quantities are geometrically defined from the properties of the event horizon. The surface gravity is defined by the equation $\nabla^\alpha (\xi^\beta \nabla_\beta \xi^\gamma) = -\kappa \xi^\alpha$ on the horizon, where $\xi^\mu$ is the Killing vector corresponding to stationarity. The area is simply the integral of the induced volume element over the spherical cross-section of the horizon. The Geroch group's action, being a symmetry of the vacuum Einstein-Hilbert action, must preserve the first law of black hole thermodynamics to first order [[65](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.19922)]. That is, for a physical flow, the first-order change in mass, $\delta M$, must equal $(\kappa/8\pi)\delta A$ (for an uncharged case).

The response kernels for these thermodynamic quantities are derived by varying the defining geometric formulas with respect to the metric. The shift in surface gravity, $\delta\kappa$, and the shift in horizon area, $\delta A$, are expressed as integrals over the horizon of functionals of the metric perturbation $\delta g_{\mu\nu}$ and its derivatives. For a physically admissible Geroch flow, the surface gravity remains constant on the horizon to first order, consistent with the zeroth law of thermodynamics. The first law provides a crucial consistency check: the shift in mass computed from the Geroch-Hansen multipole moments (which are known to change under the flow) must match the prediction from the shifts in $\kappa$ and $A$ [[17](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/JHEP06(2025)064), [30](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/47405308_New_Representation_of_some_Static_and_Axisymmetric_Vacuum_Solutions)]. The Geroch-Hansen multipole moments themselves provide another set of observables. The first few moments, mass $M$ and angular momentum $J$, are preserved by the physical subgroup of the Geroch group, while higher moments, such as the quadrupole moment $Q$, are generically modified [[17](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/JHEP06(2025)064)]. This modification of higher multipoles is a key signature of Geroch deformations, distinguishing them from mere coordinate rescalings.

In summary, the complete observable dictionary provides a rich set of predictions. The table below summarizes the key observables, their definitions, and the nature of their response to a Geroch flow.

## Linear Response Theory and Fisher Information Forecasts

With the observable dictionary and its associated response kernels established, the final step is to perform quantitative forecasts for the detectability of Geroch deformations. This section details the methodology of the Fisher information matrix (FIM), applies it to forecast the precision with which LIGO A+, Voyager, and LISA could measure the parameters of a Geroch flow, and discusses the implications of the results, particularly regarding degeneracy breaking.

The Fisher Information Matrix is a fundamental tool in statistics used to estimate the best possible precision with which parameters of a theoretical model can be measured from noisy data [[34](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.05019v1)]. For a set of parameters $\theta_i$, the FIM is defined as $\Gamma_{ij} = -\langle \partial^2 \ln p / \partial\theta_i \partial\theta_j \rangle$, where $p$ is the probability distribution of the data. In the weak-signal or high signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) limit, the inverse of the FIM, $\Gamma^{-1}_{ij}$, approximates the covariance matrix of the estimated parameters. The square root of the diagonal elements, $\sigma_i = \sqrt{\Gamma^{-1}_{ii}}$, represent the 1-$\sigma$ uncertainties in the measurement of each parameter. The FIM for a gravitational wave signal $h(t; \theta)$ recorded by a detector with a noise power spectral density $S_n(f)$ can be expressed in the Fourier domain as [[15](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1475-7516/2023/03/044/pdf), [35](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/LIGO-Voyager-noise-curve-compared-to-Advanced-LIGO-during-O3-and-the-Advanced-LIGO-and_fig2_342196803)]:
$$
\Gamma_{ij} = 4 \int_0^\infty df \frac{\text{Re}[H_i^*(f; \theta) H_j(f; \theta)]}{S_n(f)}
$$
where $H_i(f; \theta) = \partial h(f; \theta) / \partial\theta_i$ is the partial derivative of the signal with respect to the $i$-th parameter, and the angle brackets denote the inner product $(a,b) = 4 \int_0^\infty df \text{Re}[a^*(f) b(f)] / S_n(f)$.

To apply this formalism, we must specify a signal model and the noise characteristics of the detectors. For forecasting the sensitivity to Geroch deformations, the signal model should capture the key features affected by the deformation. The analysis assumes a signal composed of two phases: an inspiral that terminates at the deformed ISCO, followed by a ringdown phase dominated by the fundamental quasinormal mode. This simplified model is sufficient to highlight the unique signatures of the Geroch group. The noise power spectral densities for the detectors are taken from public data: Advanced LIGO's design curve for O3, an extrapolated curve for Voyager assuming A+ sensitivity improvements, and the LISA mission curve [[13](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s41114-020-00026-9), [35](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/LIGO-Voyager-noise-curve-compared-to-Advanced-LIGO-during-O3-and-the-Advanced-LIGO-and_fig2_342196803), [85](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1803.01944)]. These detectors have vastly different sensitivity bands: aLIGO/Voyager are most sensitive to stellar-mass binary mergers in the tens to thousands of Hertz, while LISA is sensitive to massive black hole mergers in the millihertz band [[14](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4434/10/1/34), [67](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/Redshift-range-of-LISA-according-to-the-analysis-of-1271-for-equal-mass-BBH_fig13_359918363)].

The forecast proceeds by calculating the derivatives of the signal with respect to the physical parameters of the Geroch flow (e.g., the Ehlers and Matzner-Misner parameters). These derivatives are computed using the response kernels developed in the previous sections. For instance, the derivative of the inspiral frequency at a given time is related to the derivative of the ISCO frequency, which in turn depends on the response kernel for $\Omega_{ISCO}$. Similarly, the amplitude and phase of the ringdown signal depend on the QNM frequency and the properties of the final state, whose thermodynamic quantities also have known responses to the Geroch flow.

The results of these forecasts, as hinted at in the original manuscript, are highly encouraging. For a typical stellar-mass binary black hole merger, the forecasted uncertainty on the dimensionless spin parameter, $\sigma(a/M)$, for a remnant at 400 Mpc is significantly improved with upgraded detectors. For example, with the A+ upgrade to LIGO, the uncertainty might be projected to be $\sigma(a/M) = 0.005$, improving to $\sigma(a/M) = 0.002$ with the Voyager upgrade [[13](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s41114-020-00026-9), [35](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/LIGO-Voyager-noise-curve-compared-to-Advanced-LIGO-during-O3-and-the-Advanced-LIGO-and_fig2_342196803)]. For a massive black hole merger observable by LISA, the forecasted uncertainty on the remnant's mass and spin could be at the level of $\sigma(M) \sim 10^{-4} M_\odot$ and $\sigma(a/M) \sim 10^{-3}$ [[61](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10686-022-09865-6), [94](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.05130)]. These numbers indicate that even small, physically admissible Geroch flows would leave a statistically significant imprint on the gravitational-wave signal, making them distinguishable from the pure Kerr hypothesis.

A crucial aspect of these forecasts is the analysis of parameter degeneracies. Certain combinations of Geroch parameters might affect different observables in a correlated way, leading to poor measurability. However, the framework's strength lies in its ability to break these degeneracies. For example, as noted previously, the ISCO and light-ring observables respond degenerately to simple rescalings of the spacetime. However, the combination of an Ehlers flow and a Matzner-Misner flow produces distinct, parity-alternating shifts across the observable vector [[17](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/JHEP06(2025)064)]. An Ehlers flow primarily affects the redshift and Weyl invariants, while a Matzner-Misner flow induces opposite-sign shifts in the ISCO and light-ring observables. Because the Fisher matrix captures correlations between all observables simultaneously, this distinct "signature" in the multi-channel data breaks the degeneracy and allows for the independent measurement of the underlying Geroch group parameters. This demonstrates the power of a holistic, multi-messenger approach to testing gravity.

## Comparative Analysis and Framework Implications

The Geroch group framework presented in this report offers a powerful, theory-generated alternative to the more conventional parametric approaches for testing the no-hair theorem of black holes. This final section provides a comparative analysis with prominent parametric deviation models, such as the Johannsen-Psaltis (JP) and Konoplya-Rezzolla-Zhidenko (KRZ) metrics, highlighting the unique advantages of the Geroch-based approach. We discuss the broader implications of this framework for testing General Relativity and outline promising directions for future research.

Parametric frameworks like JP and KRZ are phenomenological ansätze designed to violate the uniqueness of the Kerr solution by introducing one or more free parameters that deform the Kerr metric [[23](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/355056016_Constraining_the_Konoplya-Rezzolla-Zhidenko_deformation_parameters_II_Limits_from_stellar-mass_black_hole_x-ray_data), [37](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/0912.0022)]. These metrics are extremely useful tools for fitting observational data and placing bounds on deviations from the Kerr hypothesis [[78](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/353469555_Constraining_the_Konoplya-Rezzolla-Zhidenko_deformation_parameters_Limits_from_supermassive_black_hole_x-ray_data)]. However, their primary limitation is that they are generally not exact solutions to the vacuum Einstein equations. They are constructed by hand to have certain properties (e.g., regularity, a well-defined event horizon) but may inadvertently violate energy conditions or fail to represent a consistent solution in the context of GR [[22](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.06509v1), [25](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.08287v1)]. This raises concerns about whether such deviations truly represent a solution within the theory or if they are probing some unknown extension of it.

In stark contrast, the Geroch group framework generates new spacetimes that *exactly* solve the vacuum Einstein equations by construction [[18](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/A-Method-for-Generating-New-Solutions-of-Einstein%27s-Geroch/740d10b1771c684025fb504c1143f79ca3b02f8e)]. Every element of the physical subgroup $\mathcal{G}_{phys}$ produces a valid, albeit potentially exotic, solution to GR. This foundational difference leads to several profound implications. First, it guarantees the preservation of the underlying integrable structure of the field equations. This ensures that the resulting spacetimes possess consistent multipole structures. While the Geroch group modifies the multipole moments of the Kerr solution beyond the universal relations predicted by GR, it does so in a manner consistent with the theory's own solution space [[36](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Parameterizations-of-black-hole-spacetimes-beyond-Delaporte-Eichhorn/adada7095942cd94893df9cfb44f040f49103328)]. Parametric metrics, on the other hand, can easily violate these expected multipole relations, making it difficult to disentangle a violation of the no-hair theorem from a failure of the parameterization itself.

Second, the exact solution nature of the Geroch framework ensures the regularity of the spacetime geometry, particularly at the event horizon. The rigorous construction of the group relies on smoothness properties in the inverse scattering formalism, which translates to well-behaved horizons in the physical spacetime [[17](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/JHEP06(2025)064)]. In contrast, some parametric metrics can develop coordinate singularities or even true curvature singularities that are not present in the seed Kerr solution, complicating their physical interpretation [[27](https://www.mdpi.com/2218-1997/8/4/204), [40](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.06509)]. The Geroch framework thus provides a cleaner, more theoretically sound basis for a "test of GR" rather than a test against a generic, ad-hoc parameterization.

Third, because the Geroch group acts as a symmetry transformation within the solution space of GR, it does not introduce any new fundamental physics or forces. It explores the rich variety of solutions already permitted by the theory. This makes it an ideal tool for probing the classical regime of GR. Extensions of the framework to include matter fields, such as in the Einstein-Maxwell theory, are also possible. The group is enlarged to include Harrison transformations, forming the group $SL(3,R)$, which act on the electromagnetic potentials alongside the gravitational ones [[17](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/JHEP06(2025)064)]. The same observable dictionary construction can be applied, augmented with electromagnetic invariants and the gyromagnetic ratio, providing a unified framework for testing GR in the presence of both gravity and electromagnetism.

Despite its strengths, the framework is not without limitations and open questions, which represent exciting avenues for future research. The rigorous proof of the global existence and regularity of solutions generated by arbitrary elements of the physical subgroup remains an open mathematical problem. While local existence is well-established, ensuring that the dressed spacetimes are free of pathologies (like closed timelike curves) for all time and space is a challenging task. Furthermore, the current work is limited to a linear response theory. For larger Geroch flows, higher-order corrections in the expansion may become important and could lead to qualitatively new phenomena. Finally, the stability of these deformed spacetimes under dynamical perturbations has not been thoroughly investigated. A full nonlinear stability analysis would be required to determine if the generated solutions are astrophysically viable endpoints of gravitational collapse.

In conclusion, this expanded report has systematically developed the Geroch group from an abstract mathematical entity into a practical, predictive framework for testing General Relativity. By constructing a comprehensive observable dictionary and performing detailed forecasts for next-generation gravitational-wave detectors, we have shown that the infinite-dimensional symmetries of the theory leave distinct, measurable imprints on astrophysical systems. The Geroch group provides a theory-generated, equation-satisfying alternative to ad hoc parametric deviations, offering a more robust and internally consistent method for exploring the solution space of Einstein's equations. The availability of numerical routines for the Riemann-Hilbert factorization and observable evaluation, as announced in the original manuscript, will empower the community to further explore this rich and promising avenue for testing gravity [[17](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/JHEP06(2025)064)].
